AI WRITING RULES FOR AN IACR FLAGSHIP-CONFERENCE PAPER
(CRYPTO / EUROCRYPT style)

PURPOSE

Use these rules whenever drafting, revising, or reviewing this paper. The target
voice is the one common to strong papers at flagship cryptography conferences:
precise, theorem-driven, economical, self-critical, and easy to audit. Prefer
clarity and correctness over sounding sophisticated.

These are writing rules, not the venue's formatting instructions. Check the
current call for papers and author instructions separately before submission.


1. NON-NEGOTIABLE PRINCIPLES

1. State only what the manuscript actually establishes. Never turn a conjecture,
   heuristic, numerical transition, first-moment calculation, or empirical
   observation into a theorem-level claim.

2. Label the status of every important statement clearly. Use words such as
   "we prove," "we show under Assumption X," "we compute," "our experiments
   suggest," "we conjecture," or "it remains open." Do not blur these categories.

3. Put the main result, its assumptions, and its limitations where readers can
   find them quickly: the abstract, introduction, and formal theorem statement.

4. Optimize for a skeptical expert reader. Make definitions complete, expose
   parameter dependencies, identify failure cases, and explain why each proof
   step is valid.

5. Do not imitate prestige with inflated language. A top-paper voice is confident
   because the statements are specific and supported, not because it uses words
   such as "groundbreaking," "remarkable," "obviously," or "clearly."

6. Never invent citations, theorem names, historical claims, performance data,
   security reductions, parameter values, or comparisons. Mark missing evidence
   with a visible TODO rather than filling the gap speculatively.


2. THE DEFAULT VOICE

- Write in direct, formal English.
- Use active voice when it identifies responsibility: "We prove Lemma 4" and
  "Algorithm A samples the permutation."
- Use the present tense for facts in the paper: "Theorem 2 bounds the distance."
- Use the past tense only for actions completed in an experiment or prior work.
- Prefer short declarative sentences, especially around definitions and claims.
- Put the main point of a paragraph in its first sentence.
- Keep one logical purpose per paragraph.
- Prefer concrete nouns and verbs to abstract filler.
- Use "we" for author actions; do not use "I" or address the reader as "you."
- Use "the adversary" or a named algorithm, not anthropomorphic phrasing.
- Use "simple" or "immediate" only when the supporting step is genuinely routine
  for the expected audience; otherwise give the argument.

Preferred:
  "For every fixed rate R < 1/2, the ensemble has relative distance at least
  delta(R) with probability 1 - o(1)."

Avoid:
  "It is easy to see that our powerful framework achieves excellent distance."


3. ABSTRACT: A FIVE-PART CONTRACT WITH THE READER

Write the abstract as a compact sequence containing all of the following:

1. Context and problem: one or two sentences explaining the exact problem and
   why it matters to cryptography.
2. Gap: what was not known or what obstacle prevented a satisfactory result.
3. Result: the strongest proved claim, including the relevant asymptotic regime,
   assumptions, security notion, or parameter restrictions.
4. Technique: the main new construction, proof idea, or analytical device.
5. Consequence and limitation: what the result enables, plus an important caveat
   if the theorem is conditional, partial, or does not cover a desired regime.

Abstract rules:

- Do not begin with generic claims such as "Cryptography is important."
- Do not include a literature survey.
- Do not cite unless a citation is essential to identify a specific prior result.
- Do not advertise a broader result than the formal theorems support.
- Distinguish asymptotic results from concrete parameters.
- If evidence is numerical rather than proved, say so explicitly.


4. INTRODUCTION: TELL A TECHNICAL STORY

The introduction should answer, in this order:

1. What exact problem is being solved?
2. Why does a cryptographer care?
3. Why is the problem technically difficult?
4. What was known before this work?
5. What does this paper prove?
6. What is genuinely new in the construction or proof?
7. What remains unresolved?

Use the following structure unless the paper strongly demands another one:

- Motivation and problem statement.
- Technical obstacle or failure of the natural approach.
- Informal description of the construction/result.
- Contributions, as a short numbered list.
- Technical overview of the proof.
- Limitations and scope.
- Organization of the paper, only if it helps navigation.

Each contribution bullet must contain:

- a precise object or setting;
- an action verb such as "construct," "prove," "reduce," "characterize," or
  "evaluate";
- the quantitative or qualitative guarantee;
- the assumptions or regime; and
- a pointer to the corresponding theorem or section when practical.

Do not list routine exposition, standard definitions, or ordinary implementation
work as a primary contribution. Do not claim "the first" or "state of the art"
without a careful and supportable comparison.


5. RELATED WORK: COMPARE, DO NOT CATALOGUE

- Organize prior work by the technical question it addresses, not chronologically.
- State what each cited line of work achieves and under which model or parameters.
- Explain the exact difference from this paper: construction, assumption,
  security notion, rate, field size, distance, complexity, or proof technique.
- Give credit generously and neutrally.
- Compare like with like. Do not compare an asymptotic theorem with a concrete
  benchmark, or a heuristic estimate with a proved bound, without flagging the
  distinction.
- Avoid vague dismissal: never write "prior work is inefficient" without naming
  the metric, regime, and evidence.
- Prefer a compact comparison table when several works differ along the same
  three or more dimensions.
- Cite primary sources for technical results. Use surveys for orientation, not as
  a substitute when attributing a theorem.


6. DEFINITIONS, MODELS, AND NOTATION

- Define every nonstandard object before first substantive use.
- State probability spaces explicitly: what is sampled, in what order, and which
  quantities are fixed.
- State all quantifiers. Distinguish "for every code," "with high probability over
  the ensemble," and "there exists a code."
- Define the asymptotic variable and what remains fixed as it grows.
- State the base of logarithms and conventions for entropy, statistical distance,
  negligible functions, and field/vector notation.
- Give dimensions and domains for maps, matrices, codes, and random variables.
- Use one symbol for one concept and one concept for one symbol.
- Do not overload n, k, q, R, or lambda across incompatible roles.
- Include a notation table if the notation cannot be recalled locally.
- Identify boundary cases: zero weight, empty support, degenerate rate, small
  block count, and field-characteristic restrictions.

For coding claims used in cryptography, state at least:

- alphabet/field and linearity assumptions;
- block length, dimension, rate, and distance notion;
- whether parameters are exact, asymptotic, expected, or experimental;
- the code ensemble and its randomness;
- encoding/decoding complexity when relevant; and
- the precise cryptographic property the coding result is intended to support.


7. THEOREMS, LEMMAS, AND CLAIMS

Every major theorem should be understandable in isolation. Its statement must
include or point unambiguously to:

- the construction or algorithm;
- assumptions and parameter range;
- all randomness and quantifiers;
- the guarantee, with explicit dependence on parameters;
- the success/failure probability;
- the security or complexity model, if applicable; and
- asymptotic qualifiers.

Rules for formal claims:

- Put the strongest clean theorem early, then refine it later if necessary.
- Separate correctness, security, efficiency, and parameter claims when their
  proofs or assumptions differ.
- Do not hide a decisive restriction only in the proof.
- Do not use undefined phrases such as "good code," "efficient," "large field,"
  or "high probability" in a formal statement.
- If a constant can be computed but is omitted, say why. If it cannot currently
  be made explicit, do not imply otherwise.
- Name assumptions consistently and discuss their plausibility and provenance.
- Follow each central theorem with a short plain-language interpretation that
  does not strengthen the formal statement.


8. PROOFS: MAKE THE ARGUMENT AUDITABLE

Begin a long proof with a roadmap that identifies the main steps and the new idea.
Then write the proof so that each paragraph performs one inferential step.

- State which randomness is fixed and which is averaged over.
- Name the event being bounded.
- Justify changes in the order of sums, expectations, limits, and maxima.
- Track parameter dependencies throughout big-O, little-o, and negligible terms.
- State when constants depend on rate, field size, block size, or security
  parameter.
- Check uniformity before using an asymptotic estimate inside a union bound.
- Distinguish equality, inequality, approximation, and heuristic equivalence.
- Cite the exact lemma when applying a nontrivial fact.
- Explain why each lemma's hypotheses hold in the current setting.
- Treat edge cases explicitly rather than relying on a generic argument that
  silently excludes them.
- End a proof by connecting the derived bound to the theorem's exact conclusion.

Move only genuinely routine algebra to an appendix. Keep the central proof idea
and the step that controls the dominant error term in the main body.


9. SECURITY ARGUMENTS AND REDUCTIONS

When the paper makes a cryptographic claim:

- State the security game or ideal functionality before the theorem.
- Specify adversary capabilities, corruption model, setup, and trust assumptions.
- Distinguish computational, statistical, and perfect security.
- State whether security is selective/adaptive, static/dynamic, and
  stand-alone/composable when those distinctions apply.
- Give the reduction's running time, query complexity, and advantage loss.
- Account for abort probabilities and bad events explicitly.
- Use hybrid arguments with named hybrids and one change per transition.
- State which assumption justifies each indistinguishability step.
- Do not call an assumption "standard" unless that characterization is well
  supported; name it precisely either way.
- Separate functional correctness from security.


10. NUMERICAL AND EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE

- State the purpose of each computation: validation, exploration, parameter
  selection, or performance measurement.
- Give enough information to reproduce it: algorithm, parameter grid, precision,
  software/version, hardware when relevant, and stopping criteria.
- Report units and define every axis and column.
- State whether a number is exact, rounded, estimated, or simulated.
- Include uncertainty or numerical error when meaningful.
- Do not infer a universal asymptotic theorem from finitely many plotted points.
- Use numerical evidence to motivate a conjecture, choose parameters, or confirm
  a proved expression—not to disguise a missing proof.
- Discuss parameter sets that fail or underperform, not only favorable examples.
- If concrete security is claimed, include every significant cost and loss.


11. FIGURES, TABLES, AND ALGORITHMS

- Every figure and table must make a claim or comparison easier to verify.
- Captions should be self-contained: state what is shown, the regime, and the
  principal takeaway without forcing the reader to search the main text.
- Define all symbols, colors, line styles, and normalization choices.
- Do not encode essential distinctions by color alone.
- Use tables for exact comparisons and plots for trends.
- Use pseudocode when implementation order, state, or sampling matters.
- Specify input, output, randomness, and failure behavior for each algorithm.


12. LANGUAGE THAT CALIBRATES CLAIMS

Use verbs according to the evidence:

- Formal proof: "prove," "establish," "derive," "show."
- Exact computation: "compute," "enumerate," "evaluate exactly."
- Numerical evidence: "observe numerically," "estimate," "the data suggest."
- Heuristic reasoning: "argue heuristically," "expect," "motivate."
- Open statement: "conjecture," "leave open," "do not know whether."

Avoid or replace:

- "Clearly/obviously/trivially" -> give the one-line reason.
- "It is well known" -> cite a source or state the fact precisely.
- "Significant" -> quantify the improvement or use statistical meaning correctly.
- "Optimal" -> specify the comparison class and matching lower bound.
- "Practical" -> provide a cost model and concrete measurements.
- "Secure" -> name the security definition and assumptions.
- "Negligible" -> identify the security parameter and bound.
- "With high probability" -> state the probability or define the convention.
- "Large/small" -> give a threshold or asymptotic relation.
- "Novel/new" -> describe the technical difference instead.
- "We believe" -> state whether the sentence is a conjecture or interpretation.


13. LATEX AND MATHEMATICAL PRESENTATION

- Introduce notation immediately before it becomes useful.
- Punctuate displayed equations as parts of sentences.
- Give equation numbers only to expressions referenced later.
- Use semantic macros for recurring objects and operators.
- Use consistent delimiters, transpose notation, vector style, and probability
  notation throughout.
- Break long derivations at meaningful logical steps and annotate the non-obvious
  transitions.
- Do not use raw Unicode mathematical symbols in LaTeX source when a stable LaTeX
  command is appropriate.
- Keep theorem names descriptive and consistent.
- Resolve every warning about undefined references or citations before submission.
- Use nonbreaking spaces for references such as "Theorem~3" and "Section~4."


14. SPECIAL RULES FOR THIS PAPER'S LIKELY CLAIM TYPES

For ensemble and distance results:

- Distinguish the expected weight enumerator from the probability that a random
  code has a low-weight codeword.
- Show how the first-moment or union bound yields the claimed probability result.
- Separate sparse-weight, linear-weight, and boundary regimes.
- State whether estimates are uniform over all weights in the summed range.
- Do not infer minimum distance merely from a numerical exponent unless all
  finite-length, discretization, and tail terms are controlled.

For accumulator and block-code constructions:

- Specify the permutation/interleaver distribution.
- Specify termination, boundary state, and zero-symbol conventions.
- Separate support evolution from value cancellation over larger fields.
- State when an argument is binary-only and what fails over general fields.
- Explain how local block properties combine with the global accumulator analysis.

For cryptographic motivation:

- State exactly where the code is used in the protocol or primitive.
- Explain which code property is required and whether the paper proves it.
- Do not imply a complete protocol result from an isolated coding theorem.
- If the application remains prospective, describe it as motivation, not as an
  achieved cryptographic construction.


15. EDITING RULES FOR AN AI

When asked to revise text:

1. Preserve mathematical meaning unless explicitly asked to change the result.
2. Never silently strengthen a claim.
3. Flag a claim whose support cannot be located in the manuscript.
4. Flag inconsistent notation rather than guessing which version is intended.
5. Keep citations attached to the claims they support.
6. Remove repetition, throat-clearing, meta-commentary, and promotional language.
7. Prefer a precise sentence over a longer sentence with more qualifications.
8. Split sentences containing multiple independent claims.
9. Maintain consistent terminology across abstract, introduction, theorem
   statements, captions, and conclusion.
10. When compressing, retain assumptions, quantifiers, parameter regimes, and
    negative results.
11. Do not rewrite a proof until its logical dependencies are understood.
12. If a passage mixes theorem, evidence, and conjecture, separate them visibly.


16. FINAL PAPER CHECKLIST

Before treating a draft as submission-ready, verify:

[ ] The abstract's strongest claim matches a formal theorem.
[ ] Every contribution points to evidence in the paper.
[ ] All assumptions and models are stated before they are used.
[ ] Quantifiers, probability spaces, and asymptotic variables are explicit.
[ ] Security claims name the definition, adversary model, and assumptions.
[ ] Numerical claims are reproducible and are not presented as proofs.
[ ] Comparisons use compatible models, parameters, and cost measures.
[ ] Limitations and unresolved cases are stated candidly.
[ ] The central proof idea appears in the main body.
[ ] Notation is consistent and all symbols are defined.
[ ] No citation, cross-reference, figure, table, or TODO is unresolved.
[ ] The conclusion summarizes established results rather than adding new claims.
[ ] The title is specific and supported by the paper's actual scope.
[ ] The paper complies with the current venue's page, formatting, anonymity,
    artifact, ethics, and submission requirements.


ONE-SENTENCE STANDARD

Write so that a skeptical cryptography expert can identify the exact claim, its
assumptions, its evidence, its novelty relative to prior work, and its limitations
without having to infer any of them.
